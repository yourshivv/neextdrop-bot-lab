Backtest research: 3 years, 124 coins

Before any bot went on paper trading, its rules were replayed on three years of past prices. This page shows that research: what worked, what only looked like it worked, and everything that failed. Backtests are easy to flatter, so the limits are listed as prominently as the results. Live paper results are on the main page.

Paper trading only. No real money is used. Simulated fills ignore slippage, latency and liquidity limits, so live results would be worse. This is research shared for feedback, not investment advice. Do not copy these trades.
Backtest and live, side by side

Why show two sets of numbers? A backtest has thousands of trades, so it is statistically strong, but the rules were shaped by people who had already seen the data. Live paper trading has only a few trades so far, but nobody has seen the future, so it is the honest test. Each answers the other's weakness. Where they agree, confidence grows. Where they disagree, that is the interesting part, and feedback on it is welcome.

How the tests were run

Period1 Oct 2023 to 28 Sep 2026. Split into Y1 (Oct 2023 to Sep 2024), Y2 (Oct 2024 to Sep 2025) and Y3 (Oct 2025 to Sep 2026). A rule is only trusted if it holds in all three years.
Coins124 USDT perpetual futures coins (top Binance volume, also tradable on the target exchange). 74 have the full 3 years, the rest start at their listing date. Results on the 79 newer coins were close to the 45 older ones, so the coin list is not what drives the results.
Candles1-hour and 5-minute candles. 4-hour and daily charts are built from the 1-hour ones. Exits are walked candle by candle, and when a candle touches both stop and target, the stop is assumed to hit first (the pessimistic choice).
Costs0.059% taker fee per side plus 0.05% slippage on every market fill. Slippage is the gap between the price you expect and the price you get.
RulesEach bot's rules are copied from its live strategy file, then tested without changes.
SlotsA bot can hold at most 10 trades at once, filled first come, first served, as the live bot would. Results with no slot limit are labelled as such.
Engine checkOn the 45 older coins over the last 12 months, Breakout 4h gave +0.150R in this engine and +0.16R in an earlier test on another exchange's data. The two engines agree.
Terms used on this page
R multiple
Result divided by the amount risked (the distance to the stop). +1R means the trade made what it risked. Using R makes trades of different sizes comparable.
Random-entry control
Over these 3 years most altcoins fell, so random shorts made about +0.10R per trade and random longs about -0.07R. A rule only has an edge if it beats random entries on the same side with the same exits.
In-sample vs unseen
In-sample data is what a rule was designed on. Unseen (out-of-sample) data was held back. A rule that only works in-sample is probably overfitted, meaning it memorised noise.
Market mood
A daily filter. Weak: fewer than 35% of coins above their 20-day average price (EMA20) and BTC below its own. Hot: more than 65% above and BTC above.
Drawdown
The biggest fall from a previous peak of the equity curve. In R terms it says how deep the worst stretch was.
Compounding
Risking a % of the current account, so size grows after wins. It multiplies gains, and also the size of the worst drop.

Breakout 4h on paper since 17 Oct 2026

Enters when price breaks a 20-candle high or low on the 4-hour chart, long and short. It skips every trade when the market mood is weak, and skips longs when the mood is hot. Fixed target at 6R, 10-day time limit. This is the only bot that was above zero over the full 3 years.

Live paper so far (since 2026-10-17): 25 closed trades, win rate 52%, +16.87% on the stake, +0.19 R per trade. Too few trades to judge. Open live results

Caveats for this bot

  • The market mood rule was found on these same 3 years, so it is in-sample. The stable thresholds help, but only forward testing can settle it.
  • A later test (30 Oct) on a rebuilt engine gave smaller totals (Y3 +104R instead of +190R for the same rules), with the same direction. Compare rows only within one table.
  • Moving the stop to break-even after +2R beat the plain rules in all three years in that later test, but the strict best settings did not beat the plain rules in Y3, so the evidence is moderate, not strong.

Pullback 4h on paper since 17 Oct 2026

In a daily uptrend or downtrend, waits for the 4-hour price to dip back to its 20-period average (EMA20) and enters with a limit order. Uses the same market mood filter as Breakout 4h and a 4R target. It wins only about 3 trades in 10 and relies on big winners.

Live paper so far (since start): 34 closed trades, win rate 44%, +10.67% on the stake, +0.12 R per trade. Too few trades to judge. Open live results

Caveats for this bot

  • Drawdowns are deep for the profit (-117R). Compounding is not safe here: 6R at 2% risk per trade fell 95% at its worst.
  • Monthly results correlate 0.39 with Breakout 4h, so running both gives only partial diversification. Correlation measures how much two results move together: 1 is lockstep, 0 is independent.
  • Only 28% to 30% of trades win. Long losing runs are normal for this style.

  • A shorts-only bot that trades when the whole market is weak: negative for every strategy tried (Breakout 4h shorts in weak markets -0.072R). By the time everything is weak, breakdowns are late and bounce.
  • Daily-chart swing bots (five entry types plus random entries): none positive in all three years. The best earned under 0.1R per trade and depended on the year.
  • New scalper designs (5-minute dips, 15-minute scalps, fading flush candles): all lose every year. Before fees the best had a tiny edge, but fees of roughly 0.05R to 0.1R per trade turned it negative.
  • A published futures dip idea adapted to crypto: -0.14% and -0.12% per trade (long and short), profitable in 0 of 36 months.
  • A public RSI and moving-average crossover bot: -3.02% per trade with its stated settings and worse than random entries.
  • Top strategies from a public backtest leaderboard, re-run with real prices and our costs: one claimed +19,973% and made -0.14% per trade, negative in every year. Their engine assumes the order of highs and lows inside a candle and compounds all equity, which flatters tight stops and large claims.
  • Turtle Momentum 4h: not featured. On 3,521 trades the top 1% of trades produced 97% of the profit, so it is too fragile to call a result, and every profit-protection variant tested (trailing stops, partial exits) was worse than doing nothing in every year.

Limits of this research

  • In-sample risk: the market mood filter and the 6R exit were designed on this data. The exit was picked on Y1 and Y2 and judged on Y3, which is better than nothing, but Y3 was still part of the research.
  • Selection bias: the pullback family was picked from about six ideas and several targets, and these two bots from a larger set. The best of many tries always looks better than it is.
  • Trades are not independent: one market move can trigger dozens of similar trades. 12,855 trades is not 12,855 separate pieces of evidence.
  • A weak market for altcoins: three years of mostly falling altcoins is one market regime. Rising-market behaviour is under-tested.
  • Desk safety rule: the live desk also closes all open trades when Bitcoin falls below a set level (shown as force_exit on the live page). The backtests replay each strategy's own entry and exit rules and were not built to include this rule, so live results can differ because of it.
  • Simulated fills: no order book depth, no latency, stop-first assumption. Live fills will be worse.
  • Forward test: live paper trading started on 17 Oct 2026, after the backtest data ends (28 Sep 2026). It is the first honest out-of-sample test, but it has few trades so far.
Where feedback helps most
  • How would you test whether a market-mood filter like this is overfitted? Walk-forward testing, other coins, other years?
  • Is a 6R fixed target sensible for a breakout entry, or is something smarter worth the added risk of overfitting?
  • What would you check first in a backtest that finds its best result after trying six variants?
